Decoding the Cognitive Map: A Dempster-Shafer Evidential Framework for Hippocampal Place Cell Navigation and ReplayThe Neurocomputational Foundations of Spatial Navigation and MemoryThe mammalian hippocampus acts as the primary substrate for spatial navigation, associative learning, and episodic memory consolidation. The cornerstone of this system is the place cell—a specialized pyramidal neuron, predominantly located in the CA1 and CA3 subfields, that exhibits spatially selective firing. As an organism explores an environment, these cells emit action potentials, or spikes, when the animal traverses specific, localized regions known as place fields. The collective, distributed spiking activity of the hippocampal place cell ensemble provides a highly dimensional, real-time representation of the animal's spatial coordinates, effectively functioning as a dynamic cognitive map of the physical world.Place cell activity is governed by the integration of multiple sensory and cognitive streams. Idiothetic (self-motion) cues, such as vestibular signals and optic flow, drive path integration—the continuous updating of position based on velocity and heading. Allothetic (external) cues, such as visual landmarks and environmental boundaries, serve to anchor and periodically recalibrate this internal map, preventing the accumulation of path integration errors. The formation and stability of these place fields rely heavily on NMDA receptor-dependent synaptic plasticity, which allows for the rapid encoding of salient environmental features within minutes of exposure, persisting for months. Recent advances in optical imaging have further revealed that place fields can form within a single trial via Behavioral Timescale Synaptic Plasticity (BTSP), a rapid induction mechanism that is tightly regulated by local feedback inhibition from Vasoactive Intestinal Peptide (VIP)-expressing interneurons.Understanding how downstream neural circuits interpret this complex spiking activity to derive actionable spatial information is the domain of neural decoding. For decades, the gold standard in decoding hippocampal activity has been the Bayesian framework, heavily popularized by Zhang, Ginzburg, McNaughton, and Sejnowski (1998). The classical Bayesian decoder computes the probability of the animal occupying a specific location given the observed population spiking activity by combining a likelihood function—typically based on the assumption that neurons act as independent Poisson spike generators—with a prior distribution based on the animal's historical spatial occupancy.While Bayesian position decoders have been instrumental in reconstructing trajectories during active navigation and identifying temporally compressed offline "replay" events, they operate under the strict axiomatic constraints of classical probability theory. Specifically, Bayesian probabilities must sum exactly to one across all mutually exclusive spatial locations. This additivity requirement introduces a severe limitation: traditional Bayesian models cannot distinguish between aleatoric uncertainty (the inherent, irreducible randomness in the physical spiking process) and epistemic uncertainty (the ignorance arising from missing data, sensor failure, out-of-distribution environments, or conflicting cues).When a classical Bayesian decoder is presented with sparse, ambiguous, or highly conflicting spike trains—a ubiquitous challenge during the ultra-fast SWRs of sleep replay or during the chaotic transition periods of rapid spatial remapping—it is mathematically forced to allocate probability mass across the spatial map. This often results in false certainty, hallucinatory trajectories, or a fragile over-reliance on the prior. To achieve a mathematically robust representation of spatial cognition that explicitly handles ignorance and conflict, a generalization of Bayesian probability is required.The Dempster-Shafer Theory of EvidenceThe Dempster-Shafer (DS) theory of evidence, originally developed by Arthur P. Dempster and subsequently formalized by Glenn Shafer, provides a powerful mathematical framework for reasoning with partial, uncertain, and conflicting information. As a generalization of the Bayesian theory of subjective probability, DS theory abandons the additivity axiom, enabling computational models to explicitly quantify ignorance and uncommitted belief.Unlike classical probability theory, which maps probability mass directly to the mutually exclusive singleton elements of a sample space, DS theory maps probabilities to the power set of the sample space. This allows the framework to assign belief not just to individual locations, but to sets of locations, or even to the entire environment simultaneously, perfectly capturing the ambiguity of a broadly tuned or unreliable sensor.Mathematical Foundations of the Evidential FrameworkLet the frame of discernment, denoted as $\Omega$, represent the set of all mutually exclusive and exhaustive spatial locations in a binned experimental environment. For a two-dimensional spatial arena, $\Omega = \{v_1, v_2, \dots, v_K\}$, where each $v_k = (x, y)$ is a discrete spatial bin. The power set $2^\Omega$ contains all possible subsets of $\Omega$, ranging from the empty set $\emptyset$ to the full set $\Omega$.The foundational metric in DS theory is the Basic Probability Assignment (BPA), also known as the mass function $m$. The mass function is a mapping from $2^\Omega$ to the interval $[0, 1]$. To be valid, the mass function must satisfy two core conditions:$m(\emptyset) = 0$ (no mass is assigned to the impossible event).$\sum_{A \subseteq \Omega} m(A) = 1$ (the total mass assigned across all subsets equals one).The value $m(A)$ represents the proportion of available evidence that strictly and exclusively supports the claim that the true state belongs to the subset $A$, and to no smaller subset contained within $A$. Crucially, the mass assigned to the entire frame of discernment, $m(\Omega)$, directly quantifies the degree of total ignorance, epistemic uncertainty, or uncommitted belief.From the mass function, two bounding measures are derived to quantify the true state of knowledge regarding any subset $A$:Belief ($Bel(A)$): The sum of all masses strictly contained within $A$. It represents the lower bound of certainty, reflecting the evidence that unconditionally supports $A$ 1 .
$$Bel(A) = \sum_{B \subseteq A} m(B)$$Plausibility ($Pl(A)$): The sum of all masses that intersect with $A$. It represents the upper bound of possibility, reflecting the extent to which the available evidence fails to refute $A$.
$$Pl(A) = \sum_{B \cap A \neq \emptyset} m(B) = 1 - Bel(\neg A)$$The interval $[Bel(A), Pl(A)]$ formally defines the uncertainty regarding subset $A$. The width of this interval, $Pl(A) - Bel(A)$, directly quantifies the epistemic ignorance regarding that specific hypothesis. When sufficient evidence exists to eliminate all epistemic uncertainty, the interval collapses ($Bel(A) = Pl(A)$), and the DS model devolves into a classical Bayesian probability formulation.Analytical DimensionClassical Bayesian FrameworkDempster-Shafer Evidential FrameworkDomain of Probability AssignmentSingletons in the sample space ($\Omega$)The power set of the sample space ($2^\Omega$)Representation of Complete IgnoranceForced uniform distribution (Maximum Entropy)Total mass assigned to the frame: $m(\Omega) = 1$Uncertainty QuantificationPoint estimate (Single probability value)Interval estimate ($[Bel(A), Pl(A)]$)Handling of Conflicting DataNormalization artificially obscures conflictConflict factor ($\mathcal{K}$) explicitly isolates disagreementPrimary Fusion MechanismBayes' Theorem (Prior multiplication)Dempster's Rule of Combination (Orthogonal Sum)Optimal Use CaseEnvironments with known distributions and aleatoric noiseEnvironments with out-of-distribution data, sparsity, and epistemic gapsDempster's Rule of CombinationWhen multiple independent sources of evidence are available—such as the simultaneous, independent spiking activity of multiple hippocampal place cells within a neural ensemble—their respective mass functions are fused using Dempster's Rule of Combination, denoted by the orthogonal sum operator ($\oplus$).For two independent mass functions $m_1$ and $m_2$, the combined mass for any subset $C \neq \emptyset$ is calculated by taking the intersection of all focal elements from both sources:$$m_{1 \oplus 2}(C) = \frac{1}{1 - \mathcal{K}} \sum_{A \cap B = C} m_1(A) m_2(B)$$The term $\mathcal{K}$ is the conflict factor, which measures the total mass assigned to the empty set due to fundamentally disjoint or contradictory evidence between the two sources:$$\mathcal{K} = \sum_{A \cap B = \emptyset} m_1(A) m_2(B)$$This conflict factor is highly valuable in computational neuroscience. During spatial navigation, a high $\mathcal{K}$ value indicates a fundamental mismatch between the animal's internal cognitive map and the immediate sensory representation. This serves as a mathematically rigorous biomarker for rapid spatial remapping, out-of-field firing, or the initiation of a completely novel trajectory that violates the historical occupancy prior.Derivation of Spatial Belief in a Binned EnvironmentThe core analytical task for implementing a robust evidential decoding pipeline is to derive a mathematically sound, computable expression for the Belief of a specific binned position $v = (x, y)$—denoted as $Bel(\{v\})$—relying purely on the Dempster-Shafer theory of evidence.The available neurophysiological data structures mirror those used in classical Zhang decoding:$n_i(t)$: The observed number of spikes emitted by hippocampal place cell $i$ within a specific temporal integration window $t \to t+\tau$.$\lambda_{i}(x,y)$: The empirical place ratemap for cell $i$ at position $v = (x, y)$, rigorously computed from active exploration data and expressed in units of spikes/sec.$P(x,y)$: The observed animal occupancy at position $v = (x, y)$ in seconds, normalized over the spatial domain to form a structural prior probability.The derivation of the evidential decoder requires a multi-step transformation, converting raw spike counts and ratemaps into independent basic probability assignments, followed by rigorous orthogonal fusion.Step 1: Defining the Sensor Likelihoods (Poisson Firing Dynamics)The generation of action potentials in hippocampal place cells is standardly modeled as an inhomogeneous Poisson process. For a given temporal integration window $\tau$ (typically 20 ms for active navigation decoding and 5-10 ms for compressed replay SWR decoding), the expected number of spikes for cell $i$ at a specific spatial position $v$ is $\mu_i(v) = \lambda_i(v) \tau$.The probability of observing exactly $n_i(t)$ spikes from cell $i$ during this window, given that the animal is physically located at position $v$, constitutes the Poisson likelihood function:$$L_i(v) = \frac{(\lambda_i(v)\tau)^{n_i(t)}}{n_i(t)!} \exp(-\lambda_i(v)\tau)$$In a traditional Bayesian model, the product of these likelihoods is directly multiplied by the prior $P(v)$ and subsequently normalized. However, the Bayesian assumption treats this likelihood as an absolute truth. In the Dempster-Shafer framework, the likelihood from each individual neuron $i$ acts merely as an independent source of evidence that must be translated into a Basic Probability Assignment (BPA).Step 2: Mapping Neural Likelihoods to Basic Probability AssignmentsTo harness the expressive power of DS theory, the evidence derived from cell $i$ must allocate mass to specific spatial locations while simultaneously reserving mass for ignorance. Let $\alpha_i \in (0, 1]$ represent the reliability or discount factor of cell $i$. This discount parameter allows the computational model to dynamically down-weight noisy cells, cells with highly unstable place fields, or cells exhibiting excessive out-of-field firing.For each cell $i$ in the recorded ensemble of $N$ cells, the mass assigned to a singleton position $\{v\}$ is strictly proportional to its Poisson likelihood, scaled by the reliability factor $\alpha_i$. Crucially, the remaining, uncommitted mass is assigned to the entire frame of discernment $\Omega$, explicitly modeling the sensor's epistemic uncertainty.$$m_i(\{v\}) = \alpha_i \frac{L_i(v)}{\sum_{u \in \Omega} L_i(u)}$$$$m_i(\Omega) = 1 - \alpha_i$$The implications of this formulation are profound. If a cell is deemed completely reliable ($\alpha_i = 1$), it functions identically to a classical Bayesian sensor, allocating zero mass to ignorance. However, if a cell is highly unreliable, or if it fails to fire when expected (yielding a nearly flat, low-value likelihood distribution), its effective $\alpha_i$ decreases. This shifts mass directly to $\Omega$, significantly reducing the cell's influence on the final spatial estimate without forcing the decoder to distribute erroneous, localized certainty across the environment.Step 3: Constructing the Occupancy Prior BPAThe observed animal occupancy $P(v)$ serves as the prior spatial distribution. In the DS framework, historical priors, structural constraints, or expert knowledge are treated as an initial, independent piece of evidence. We define the prior mass function $m_0$ strictly over singletons, assuming the empirical occupancy map is a robust reflection of the explorable space:$$m_0(\{v\}) = \frac{P(v)}{\sum_{u \in \Omega} P(u)}$$$$m_0(\Omega) = 0$$(Note: If the historical occupancy map was constructed with sparse, incomplete behavioral data, mass could easily be shifted to $m_0(\Omega)$ to represent uncertainty in the prior itself. For the purposes of this derivation, we assume the measured occupancy is statistically robust).Step 4: Fusing Evidence via Dempster's Rule of CombinationTo compute the final decoded spatial state, the framework must fuse the prior evidence $m_0$ with the independent evidence streams from all $N$ recorded place cells ($m_1, m_2, \dots, m_N$). This is achieved via the successive, commutative application of Dempster's Rule of Combination:$$m_{fused} = m_0 \oplus m_1 \oplus m_2 \dots \oplus m_N$$Because $m_0$ only assigns mass to singleton sets (individual spatial bins), and every subsequent $m_i$ only assigns mass to singletons and the full set $\Omega$, the intersections of focal elements during the orthogonal sum will only yield singletons (or the empty set, which is subsequently normalized out by the conflict factor $\mathcal{K}$). Consequently, the final fused mass function $m_{fused}$ will have exactly zero mass on $\Omega$ and zero mass on any subset larger than a singleton.In Dempster-Shafer theory, when the resulting mass function exclusively contains singleton focal elements, the Belief of a singleton is exactly equal to its mass: $Bel(\{v\}) = m_{fused}(\{v\})$.Expanding Dempster's rule for the intersection of the prior and the $N$ cell masses at a specific location $v$, we first calculate the unnormalized conjoint mass for $v$:$$M_{unnorm}(v) = m_0(\{v\}) \prod_{i=1}^N \left( m_i(\{v\}) + m_i(\Omega) \right)$$Substituting the expressions for the BPAs defined in Step 2 and Step 3:$$M_{unnorm}(v) = \left( \frac{P(v)}{\sum_{u \in \Omega} P(u)} \right) \prod_{i=1}^N \left[ \alpha_i \frac{L_i(v)}{\sum_{w \in \Omega} L_i(w)} + (1 - \alpha_i) \right]$$To obtain the true Belief, this unnormalized mass must be divided by the sum of all unnormalized masses across all possible spatial locations $v' \in \Omega$. This normalization intrinsically accounts for the conflict factor $\mathcal{K}$, ensuring that $1 - \mathcal{K}$ scales the distribution correctly:$$Bel(\{v\}) = \frac{ P(v) \prod_{i=1}^N \left[ \alpha_i \frac{L_i(v)}{\sum_{w \in \Omega} L_i(w)} + (1 - \alpha_i) \right] }{ \sum_{v' \in \Omega} P(v') \prod_{i=1}^N \left[ \alpha_i \frac{L_i(v')}{\sum_{w \in \Omega} L_i(w)} + (1 - \alpha_i) \right] }$$This represents the mathematically correct and rigorous expression for the Belief of a binned position $v$ given the place ratemaps, observed spikes, and occupancy data using the Dempster-Shafer framework.Analytical Reduction to Classical Bayesian DecodingA critical validation of any generalized mathematical framework is its ability to seamlessly reduce to the classical formulation under strict constraints. If we assume that every place cell in the ensemble is a perfectly reliable sensor characterized by zero epistemic uncertainty, we effectively set the discount factor $\alpha_i = 1$ for all $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$.Under this assumption of zero ignorance, the term $(1 - \alpha_i)$ vanishes entirely, and the denominator $\sum_{w \in \Omega} L_i(w)$ becomes a cell-specific scaling constant $C_i$ that is independent of the location $v$. The evidential expression simplifies to:$$Bel_{pure}(\{v\}) = \frac{ P(v) \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{L_i(v)}{C_i} }{ \sum_{v' \in \Omega} P(v') \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{L_i(v')}{C_i} }$$Since the scaling constants $C_i$ appear equally in both the numerator and the denominator, they cancel out entirely:$$Bel_{pure}(\{v\}) = \frac{ P(v) \prod_{i=1}^N L_i(v) }{ \sum_{v' \in \Omega} P(v') \prod_{i=1}^N L_i(v') }$$Substituting the original Poisson likelihood equations back into the simplified expression yields:$$Bel_{pure}(\{v\}) \propto P(v) \prod_{i=1}^N \left( \frac{(\lambda_i(v)\tau)^{n_i(t)}}{n_i(t)!} \exp(-\lambda_i(v)\tau) \right)$$Using logarithmic identities, this can be rewritten in the highly recognizable exponential form popularized by Zhang et al. (1998):$$Bel_{pure}(\{v\}) \propto P(v) \left( \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{\tau^{n_i(t)}}{n_i(t)!} \right) \exp\left( \sum_{i=1}^N n_i(t) \ln \lambda_i(v) - \tau \sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i(v) \right)$$This mathematical convergence proves definitively that the traditional Bayesian place cell decoder is simply a special, heavily constrained case of the Dempster-Shafer decoder where sensor ignorance is artificially suppressed to zero. This is a critical requirement when iteratively tuning the cell reliability parameters ($\alpha_i$) to optimize the precise balance between spatial belief and global ignorance during the design phase of the evidential decoder.Dynamics of Replay, Consolidaton, and Spatial PlanningA paramount application of spatial position decoding is the analysis of hippocampal "replay" events. Replay refers to the spontaneous reactivation of temporally ordered place cell sequences that originally represented physical trajectories experienced during active navigation. These reactivations occur predominantly during periods of wakeful immobility or slow-wave sleep, tightly synchronized with Sharp-Wave Ripples (SWRs)—high-frequency local field potential oscillations (150-250 Hz) originating in the CA3 region.Replay is widely considered the fundamental neurobiological mechanism underlying memory consolidation, reinforcement learning, and spatial planning. Research indicates that hippocampal replay actively captures the unique topological structure of novel environments, efficiently mapping bifurcations and complex maze layouts.Resolving Epistemic Uncertainty in Sparse Replay EventsStandard Bayesian decoders consistently struggle when applied to SWR replay events. Due to the extreme temporal compression of replay (often 10x to 20x faster than physical traversal), the spike count $n_i(t)$ within a short analysis window ($\tau \approx 5-20$ ms) is exceptionally sparse. This sparsity produces noisy, poorly defined likelihood distributions. Because classical Bayesian models lack a mechanism to assign mass to the frame of discernment $\Omega$, they are forced to distribute probability mass across the spatial map regardless of evidence quality. This phenomenon routinely results in jagged, discontinuous, "jumpy" trajectories that algorithms struggle to distinguish from random Poisson noise.By employing the Dempster-Shafer framework, the decoder organically mitigates this structural flaw. During a sparse temporal bin where no recorded place cell provides a strong, reliable likelihood, the unassigned mass naturally flows into $m(\Omega)$ (ignorance). Consequently, the resulting spatial $Bel(v)$ remains appropriately suppressed, and the width of the uncertainty interval $[Bel(v), Pl(v)]$ expands significantly. This explicitly signals to downstream sequence-detection algorithms (e.g., Hidden Markov Models or line-fitting heuristics) that the current temporal bin is highly uncertain. This evidential filtering prevents the computational model from hallucinating paths based on noise, yielding cleaner, statistically robust detection of both continuous and non-continuous Population Burst Events (PBEs).Forward Planning, Reverse Evaluation, and Motivational PreplayRecent theoretical frameworks, including those simulated by Pho et al., suggest that replay is not merely the rote, stochastic playback of episodic memories, but a highly dynamic operation that actively evaluates memory utility and guides prospective decision-making. During goal-directed navigation on a linear track or complex maze, awake replay frequently traces paths forward toward a known reward, acting as a prospective cognitive search algorithm evaluating future possibilities. Conversely, upon reaching a reward, reverse replay often propagates backward from the goal, serving a retrospective evaluation function critical for updating state-action values in reinforcement learning paradigms.Intriguingly, replay trajectories are not rigidly bound strictly to previously executed paths. Studies have observed "layout-conforming replay," where reactivated spatial sequences dynamically bend around newly introduced barriers or explore previously unvisited, yet visually accessible, maze arms. Furthermore, goal-biased "preplay" occurs when an animal visually observes food delivery to an unvisited portion of an environment; the hippocampus simulates trajectories to this motivationally relevant, yet unexplored space.When decoding these novel, generative trajectories, classical decoders reliant entirely on historical occupancy $P(x,y)$ face a severe, insurmountable conflict: the Poisson likelihood strongly points to an unvisited area, while the prior strictly forbids it ($P(v) \approx 0$). In DS theory, this acute conflict between the structural prior ($m_0$) and the sensory evidence ($m_{obs}$) is explicitly quantified by the conflict metric $\mathcal{K}$. High conflict indicates a fundamental mismatch between the animal's internal cognitive map and the immediate sensory representation—a signature precisely indicative of learning, map updating, or preplay exploration. Computational neuroscientists can thus use $\mathcal{K}$ as a continuous biomarker for spatial map reconfiguration and cognitive flexibility.Global Remapping, Feedback Inhibition, and Adaptive PlasticityThe spatial coding properties of the hippocampus are remarkably dynamic. When an animal is introduced to a highly novel environment, or when substantial contextual or sensory alterations are made to a familiar environment, place cells undergo a phenomenon known as "remapping". Remapping occurs in several distinct forms:Rate Remapping: The spatial location of the place field remains structurally consistent, but the peak firing rate $\lambda_{max}$ changes drastically, often to encode non-spatial variables like odor or task demands.Global Remapping: The place fields shuffle entirely, forming a completely orthogonal cognitive map where the spatial coordinates of the new fields are statistically uncorrelated with their previous positions.During the initial moments of exploration in a novel environment, the rapid induction of new place fields creates a period of representational instability. The network transitions from an unmapped or previously mapped state to a rigidly mapped state. This rapid plasticity is heavily regulated by local feedback inhibition. Specifically, VIP-expressing interneurons control hippocampal place cell remapping through transient disinhibition; manipulating these interneurons optogenetically drastically alters the dynamics of place field formation in novel environments.Analyzing neural activity during these transient, highly plastic transition periods using standard Bayesian decoders yields inherently flawed results. Because the pre-existing, familiar place ratemaps $\lambda_i(x,y)$ are no longer valid in the novel context, applying them to the new spike trains results in nonsensical spatial predictions with artificially high confidence.The Dempster-Shafer decoding framework accommodates this network-level plasticity seamlessly. As the correlation between the sensory input and the established ratemaps breaks down during global remapping, the empirical likelihoods flatten, and the cell reliability parameters $\alpha_i$ are dynamically attenuated. The system's aggregate belief mass shifts heavily from the spatial singletons $\{v\}$ into the frame of discernment $\Omega$. Consequently, the model outputs high ignorance rather than erroneous location predictions. As the new cognitive map stabilizes, VIP-mediated inhibition restabilizes the network, and new $\lambda_i(x,y)$ values are established through exploration, the epistemic uncertainty decreases, and the mass shifts back to specific spatial bins.Expanding the Frame of Discernment: Border, Grid, and Object CellsThe spatial navigation network of the mammalian brain extends far beyond the CA1 and CA3 place cells. The broader Entorhinal-Hippocampal complex includes grid cells (which exhibit periodic, hexagonally symmetric firing fields, providing a metric for space), border and boundary vector cells (which anchor to the geometric edges of the environment), and object vector cells (which fire at specific distances and directions from distinct objects).Normative models of spatial cognition suggest that place cells effectively integrate these diverse, upstream spatial representations to form the localized cognitive map. However, mathematically integrating these heterogeneous spatial sensors is a notorious challenge in computational modeling. Grid cells encode relative path integration, border cells encode absolute boundary proximity, and visual inputs encode local landmarks.Dempster-Shafer theory excels precisely at this type of heterogeneous sensor fusion. In a unified evidential model, the power set $2^\Omega$ can be evaluated flexibly by different sensory modalities. For example, a border cell might not provide evidence for a single spatial point $v$, but rather for a set of points constituting a line parallel to a physical wall. In DS theory, a mass function can assign belief directly to this geometric subset without arbitrarily distributing it across the individual constituent points.When the mass functions of place cells (localized points), border cells (lines), and grid cells (periodic arrays) are combined via Dempster's orthogonal sum, the intersection of these sets naturally isolates the true spatial location. This provides a profound mathematical formalization for how the brain might continuously rebalance idiothetic (self-motion) and allothetic (landmark) cues, fine-tuning the cognitive map even when visual cues and path integration temporarily conflict.ConclusionThe transition from classical Bayesian probability to the Dempster-Shafer theory of evidence represents a significant advancement in the methodology of neural decoding. By addressing the fundamental mathematical limitations of standard probabilistic additivity, the DS framework allows for the explicit, rigorous quantification of ignorance, epistemic uncertainty, and sensor conflict.When applied to hippocampal place cell ensembles, the DS derivation of spatial Belief ($Bel(\{v\})$) elegantly incorporates empirical place ratemaps, observed spike counts, and historical occupancy priors. By introducing cell-specific reliability discounting, the mathematical formulation inherently protects against overconfidence during noisy, temporally compressed events like sharp-wave ripple replay, or during periods of rapid synaptic plasticity and global remapping.Supported by modern computational architectures and pipeline workflows—such as those developed in pyPhoPlaceCellAnalysis—this evidential framework provides a robust, highly scalable toolset for neuroinformatics. Ultimately, deploying Dempster-Shafer theory in computational neuroscience provides researchers with a more nuanced, flexible, and mathematically rigorous window into how the mammalian brain constructs, maintains, and utilizes the cognitive map for navigation, memory, and learning.
